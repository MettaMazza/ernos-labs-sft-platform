DATA EVALUATION:

How are data evaluated?

Scientific and technical data may be examined from three viewpoints:

  • how well is the data generation described,
  • how do the data follow the known physical laws,
  • and how do the data compare to other measurements or calculations of the same phenomena.

The description of data generation is crucial. The identification and control of all relevant independent variables must be addressed and demonstrated. For mature areas such as thermodynamics and atomic physics, many measurement techniques are well characterized. In these cases, the adherence to physical laws and intercomparisons predominate. For areas in which behavior is not well understood, such as corrosion, data from different experiments are not usually comparable. Consequently, documentation of control of the experimental condition is most important.

INTRODUCTION:

The NIST WWW Structural Ceramics Database (WebSCD) provides evaluated materials property data for a wide range of advanced ceramics known variously as structural ceramics, engineering ceramics, and fine ceramics. These materials tend to have low mass densities and high strengths and tend to be resistant to corrosion. These characteristics form the basis for applications of these materials in high-temperature, energy-efficient heat exchangers, advanced engine designs, bearings, wear resistant parts, and stable electronic substrates and electronic packaging.

The range of materials covers the major series of compounds derived from the ceramic oxide, carbide, nitride, boride, and oxynitride chemical families. The materials are described by specification and characterization information that includes processing details and chemical compositions. Physical characteristics such as density and crystal structure are given in numeric tables. All measured values are evaluated and supported by descriptions of the measurement methods, procedures, and conditions. In all cases, the sources of the data are fully documented in a detailed bibliography.

General Materials Processing Notes

The authors cite T.R. Anthony, et al., Diamond and Relat. Mater., Vol. 1, 717 (1992), and summarize the procedure as follows. "Nine single-crystal diamonds, manufactured by high-pressure, temperature-gradient methods, were used in these experiments. Isotope composition ranged from nominally 0% to 99% 13C... The <111>-oriented samples were reasonably thick (1mm - 2 mm), with a typically cross-sectional area of 6 x 6 mm2 and a mass of 0.1 g - 0.2 g. The <100>-oriented samples were thinner (0.3 mm - 0.7 mm) and smaller, with typical dimensions 4 x 4 mm2. After manufacture, the diamonds were cut and polished to optical-quality finish by a professional jeweler."

Measurement Methods

Ultrasonic method
The authors cite E.P. Papadakis, J. Acoust. Soc. Am., Vol. 42, 1045 (1967), and summarize the procedure as follows. "The acoustic phase velocities were measured using a broadband pulse-echo technique derived from the pulse-echo overlap method. Our measurements were performed using a fast digital oscilloscope and a commercial ultrasonic pulser/receiver. Two different transducers were used: one generated L waves with a center frequency of 100 MHz and a nominal bandwidth of ±50 MHz, while the other generated 20 MHz linearly polarized T waves with a bandwidth of approximately ±10 MHz. An integral part of each transducer was a 0.5 in. quartz buffer rod that served to separate in time the multiple ultrasonic pulses. The diamond samples were placed directly in contact with the buffer rod using glycerin (L transducer) or honey (T transducer) to assure good acoustic coupling. Using this experimental setup, ultrasonic pulse-echo wave forms were captured with the oscilloscope. The round-trip travel time was determined from the time between like phases for several echoes in the wave form. Signal averaging and the use of several (5-10) echoes made an experimental repeatability of ±1 ns possible. The transit times were then divided into twice the sample thickness to determine the longitudinal and transverse velocities. Thicknesses were obtained by measuring the samples repeatedly using a digital micrometer with an accuracy of ±2 µm"
